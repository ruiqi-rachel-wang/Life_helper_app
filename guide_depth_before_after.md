## Renting a new place

### "Tour the unit and read the lease…"  ⟨trimmed: 6 → 4 checklist items⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> The lease is a binding contract — once you sign, its terms hold. Read every line and ask about anything unclear before you sign. If a promise isn't written in the lease, it doesn't count.
- Rent, the due date, late fees, and lease length.
- The deposit amount — and exactly what you must do to get it back.
- Which utilities you pay vs. the landlord, plus rules on pets and early-termination fees.
- No U.S. credit history? A landlord may ask for a bigger deposit, a co-signer, or a few months up front — ask what proof they'll accept instead (pay stubs, a bank letter, or an offer letter).

🔗 CFPB — renting a home

### "Pay the security deposit…"  ⟨trimmed: explanation only⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Pay by a traceable method — a check or bank transfer, not cash without a receipt — and get a dated receipt. It's your proof when it's time to get the deposit back.

### "Photograph the unit's condition…"  ⟨trimmed: 3 → 1 checklist item⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Photos of any pre-existing damage are how you get your full deposit back — the landlord can't charge you for what was already there. Take them the day you get the keys, before you move anything in.
- Note any existing damage in writing and give the landlord a copy.

### "Get renters insurance…"  ⟨trimmed: 3 → 1 checklist item⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Renters insurance covers your belongings (theft, fire, water damage) and your liability — usually $10–20 a month. Your landlord's insurance does not cover your things.
- Send proof to your landlord if the lease requires it.

### "Set up how you'll pay rent…"  ⟨trimmed: explanation only⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Set up autopay or the tenant portal so you're never late — late rent means fees, and can hurt the references you'll need for your next place. Keep proof of each payment.

## Buying a home

### "Check your credit and set a budget…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Lenders decide your rate from your credit and your debt-to-income — check your reports early so there's time to fix errors.
- Get your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com — the official, federally authorized site.
- Dispute any errors before you apply — they can cost you a better rate.
- Budget the full monthly cost: loan payment, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA fee.
- Plan the cash you'll need: the down payment (some loans start around 3%) plus 2–5% closing costs.

🔗 CFPB — credit reports and scores

### "Get pre-approved for a mortgage…"  *(reader-fix)*
**Previous build:** checklist showed, but this explanation + the CFPB link were **hidden**:
**Now (now visible):**
> Pre-approval shows sellers you're serious and sets your real budget. Compare a few lenders in the same short window — rate-shopping that way barely affects your credit.

🔗 CFPB — owning a home

### "Find a real estate agent…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> A buyer's agent represents you, not the seller. You may be asked to sign a buyer agreement — read what it says about services and fees before signing.
- Interview 2–3 agents and ask how well they know your target neighborhoods.
- Ask what their fee is and who pays it — get it in writing.
- Check their license on your state's real-estate board site.

### "Make an offer…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Your agent drafts the offer using recent sales of similar homes. The earnest-money deposit (often 1–3%) shows you're serious and counts toward your purchase at closing.
- Keep the inspection, financing, and appraisal contingencies — they let you back out safely if something goes wrong.
- Earnest money goes to an escrow or title company — never wire money based on emailed instructions without calling to verify; wire fraud is common in home sales.
- If the offer is accepted, save the signed purchase agreement.

## Starting a new job

### "Set up direct deposit…"  ⟨trimmed: 3 → 1 checklist item⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Direct deposit sends your pay straight to your bank account. You'll need your routing number and account number — both are on a check or in your banking app. Double-check them; a typo can delay your first pay.
- Have a voided check ready, if HR asks for one.

### "Check your first paycheck…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Gross pay is before deductions; net (take-home) pay is after. Check your first stub closely — pay mistakes are easiest to fix early.
- Your pay rate and hours match your offer.
- Taxes withheld — federal, state, Social Security, and Medicare.
- Any benefit or retirement deductions you signed up for.
- The money landed in the right bank account.

### "Set aside money for taxes…" *(contractor path)*
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> As a 1099 contractor, no taxes are taken out of your pay — you handle them yourself. Set aside roughly 25–30% of each payment, and expect to pay the IRS four times a year.
- Move about 25–30% of each payment into a separate savings 'tax' bucket.
- Track your income and business expenses — expenses lower what you owe.
- Pay quarterly estimated taxes (federal, and state if it has income tax).

🔗 IRS — self-employed tax center

## Car accident

### "Get medical care…" *(shown when injury is reported)*
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Adrenaline can hide injuries for hours or days — getting checked soon also ties any treatment to the accident, which the insurance claim will ask about.
- See a doctor or urgent care within a day or two, even if you feel okay.
- Tell them it was a car accident, so it's in the record.
- Keep every bill, record, and receipt — the claim can reimburse them.

### "If the car isn't drivable, arrange a tow…" *(shown when a tow is needed)*
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Call your insurer before the tow if you can — many policies include towing, and an approved shop avoids surprise fees. Don't let the car sit in a storage lot; storage fees add up daily.
- Ask your insurer about roadside/tow coverage and approved shops.
- Tow to a repair shop or your home — not a storage lot.
- Save the tow receipt and write down where the car is.

### "Get a repair estimate…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> The insurer usually inspects the damage — an adjuster visit or photos you upload — before approving repairs.
- Get a written estimate from a shop you trust.
- Don't authorize repairs before the claim is approved, unless you're paying yourself.
- Ask whether the shop can bill the insurer directly.

> _"Exchange info / photograph the scene" and "start a claim with your insurer"
> already had detail in build 2 and are unchanged._

## Get health insurance

### "A recent life change…" *(shown when a life event is reported)*
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> A Special Enrollment Period usually gives you 60 days from the life event to enroll — after that you generally wait for open enrollment, so start now.
- Qualifying changes include losing other coverage, moving, marriage, a new baby or adoption, or certain income or household changes.
- You may need to prove it — a coverage-loss letter, marriage or birth certificate, or a lease.
- Apply at HealthCare.gov and report the life change, or start a new application.

🔗 HealthCare.gov — Special Enrollment Period

### "Enroll through your job…" *(job-based path)*
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Job-based coverage usually has a short sign-up window — about 30 days from your start date. Miss it and you may wait until the company's yearly open enrollment.
- Ask HR for the benefits guide and your enrollment deadline.
- Compare the plans offered — premium, deductible, and whether your doctors are in-network.
- Add your spouse or children if you're covering them.
- Submit before the deadline and save the confirmation.

### "Have your household size and income ready…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Your household size and income decide everything — whether you qualify for Medicaid and how large your Marketplace savings are. Lawfully present immigrants can use the Marketplace and often qualify for these savings; undocumented adults generally can't, though some states cover children and pregnancy regardless of status.
- Household size and everyone's income — pay stubs or last year's tax return.
- Social Security numbers for anyone applying who has one.
- Immigration document numbers — green card, work permit (EAD), or I-94 — for anyone applying.
- A list of doctors you want to keep and prescriptions you take, to check they're covered.
- An email address to create your account.

**What you'll need:** Income for everyone in your household · Immigration document numbers
🔗 HealthCare.gov — coverage for immigrants

### "Compare plans…"
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Plans are grouped by metal level — Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum. Bronze has the lowest monthly premium but you pay more when you get care; Gold and Platinum cost more each month but less at the doctor. If your income qualifies, choose a Silver plan to unlock extra cost-sharing savings.
- Premium — what you pay every month, even with no visits.
- Deductible — what you pay yourself before the plan starts paying.
- Copay or coinsurance — your share at each visit or prescription.
- Out-of-pocket max — the most you'll pay in a year; after that the plan pays 100%.
- Check your doctors are in-network and your prescriptions are on the plan's drug list (formulary).

🔗 HealthCare.gov — how to pick a plan

### "…pay the first premium…"  ⟨trimmed: explanation only⟩ *(Marketplace path)*
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> The insurance company bills you directly for this first payment — not HealthCare.gov — so watch for that first bill (mail, email, or their site) and pay by the due date, or your start date won't stick.

### "After you enroll, save…"  ⟨trimmed: explanation only⟩
**Previous build:** No details yet.
**Now:**
> Keep your plan name, member ID, and the member-services phone number handy — they're all on your insurance card, and you'll need them at every doctor visit and pharmacy.
